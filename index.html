Warm-up & stretching

A warm-up has four jobs, in this order: raise your body temperature, open the joints you're about to use, switch on the muscles you need, then rehearse the first lift before it gets heavy. 5–8 minutes, every session, no skipping it on a rushed day — that's when a cold shoulder gets hurt.

Dynamic before, static after Everything below the workout heading is dynamic — movement-based. Save deep, held stretches for after training (see cool-down at the bottom): holding a long static stretch on a cold, unloaded muscle right before lifting can temporarily reduce the strength and power you can produce in that same session.

// 04

Training split

No bench press, and nothing that loads the front of the shoulder the way a barbell bench or a heavy overhead press does. Cables, machines and neutral grips instead — they let you build real chest, shoulder and arm size while keeping the joint on a controlled, adjustable path. Progression: once every set hits the top of its rep range with clean form, add the smallest weight increment available next session. Any pinching, sharp, or radiating pain — stop that exercise, drop the load, or swap in the regression noted.

Lean and athletic, not bodybuilder-volume Revised from a straight bodybuilder split toward a functional one: fewer isolation exercises per muscle, working weight kept lower-rep and heavier (6–10 for most compounds instead of 12–20), and every day now has one explosive or carry-based movement — kettlebell swings, box step-ups, power push-ups — full recovery between sets, no chasing a pump. Less time under the bar per session, more of it spent moving like an athlete. Direct arm/shoulder/forearm work is still in here since that's a stated goal, just trimmed to what earns its place rather than stacked three isolation moves deep.

Shoulder care

This split avoids the movements most likely to aggravate a painful shoulder, but it isn't a diagnosis or treatment. The routine below is a mobility sequence adapted from DAREBEE's rotator-cuff work — run it before every Push and Shoulders/Arms day, and ideally daily.

Move slowly, well inside a pain-free range — this builds control of the small rotator-cuff muscles, not visible size. Consistency beats intensity here.

See a physiotherapist or sports doctor if Pain is sharp or pinching rather than a dull ache · it wakes you at night · you lose strength or range lifting the arm overhead · pain radiates down toward the elbow. Any of these are worth a proper shoulder assessment before you add load — this plan can't substitute for that, and pushing through those signs is how a manageable issue becomes a structural one.

Belly & chest fat

Your segmental data flags exactly what you're describing: trunk fat is proportionally much higher than arm or leg fat on both scans. That's a genuine pattern — central fat storage is common in South Asian body types even at a "normal" BMI, and it's what's behind your visceral fat level sitting at 10, just above the 1–9 reference range on the InBody sheet.

  • Spot reduction doesn't work — no ab or chest exercise burns fat from that specific area. Overall body-fat % has to drop, and the trunk tends to be the last place it visibly leaves.
  • Visceral fat responds disproportionately well to the basics: a consistent calorie deficit, daily steps/movement (not just gym days), resistance training to protect muscle while you cut, 7–8 hours of sleep, and lower stress.
  • Cut added sugar and alcohol before cutting carbs generally — both are strongly linked to visceral fat specifically, more than total carbohydrate intake is.
  • Chest training (the incline press and cable chest press in this plan) builds and firms the muscle under the fat, which noticeably improves shape as body fat drops — but it can't remove fat tissue sitting on top of it. Expect chest shape to improve steadily alongside overall fat loss, not overnight.
  • If you ever notice a firm, coin-sized lump specifically behind the nipple rather than general softness — that's a medical question (possible gynecomastia), not a training one. Worth mentioning to a doctor, not something to try to train away.

Tracking it

  • InBody scan every 4 weeks, same time of day and similar hydration each time — it's the only measure here that separates fat loss from muscle gain.
  • Waist at the navel, fortnightly, same time of day — a better week-to-week signal than the scale.
  • Photos — front, side, back — fortnightly, same lighting and pose.
  • Training log — weight × reps on the main lift of each day, so "add the smallest increment" actually gets tracked.
  • Steps and sleep — both move visceral fat more than people expect.

Judge the block by body-fat % and visceral level first, scale weight second — your own July→August scans are the proof that's the right order.
